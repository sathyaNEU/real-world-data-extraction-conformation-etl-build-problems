# Layer 2 library: rigor patterns by domain

The client's rigor patterns by domain and task type, about 310 cards: **the pitfall and the rigorous method**, the
card's own text where it was captured in full; Shortcut: what a capable analyst does instead; Pin: what in the task
the client says makes the rigorous method the only defensible one.

- **Learn, never copy.** No pattern names a dataset or scenario. Pick one the data in hand produces and build your own
  version; the design note says in one line why this data produces it.
- **A card is a pitfall, not a stump.** Every card's method is the textbook route, and a strong solver takes textbook
  routes: blind readers landed the shipped calls whose golden was a card's method applied as the card states it, and
  missed the ones where the golden turned on a judgment the method takes for granted and the data settle (which units
  are the comparison, which exits compete, which exposure is at risk, where the curve bends, what a cap truncates). The
  card supplies the Layer 2 family and usually the default path's shortcut; the stump is priced like any other
  (`../../SKILL.md` 1.3 and 1.6a) before a card is drafted.
- **The Pin is translated, never copied.** The client's pins often name the method or the test ("the prompt names the
  model to calibrate", "a named linking convention"). Under the three rules a shipped sentence naming HOW the figure
  is computed makes the decisive step rule application (`CLAUDE.md`; `../../../determinism-check/SKILL.md` §1), so
  what a pin fixes by naming a method is filed here as the WHAT (population, outcome, window, unit, comparison, the
  horizon, the randomised or decided unit) and the design, with the data's structure ruling the shortcut out; the
  method is named only where the design or a standard's general method forces it, and never recovered from certified
  or published figures.
- **Keys.** A card's `layer2_pattern` is `<domain-key>/<task-type-key>/<n>` (the domain key in parentheses on each
  `##`, the task-type key the first token of each `###`; never renumber) or `own:<short name>` for a pattern beyond the
  library. For Quantitative Finance, Banking and Actuarial Science the client's stumping page is finer than its
  taxonomy page, so each `###` there is the stumping page's task type slugified, with the taxonomy task type (the
  card's `subdomain`, as in `client_domains` in `../../../fingerprint/vocab.json`) in parentheses; everywhere else the
  two agree. The same pattern on the same data structure is a duplicate `guard.py` blocks; on a new structure it
  warns, and the note says how this version differs.
- Domains with no patterns here (Physical & Environmental Sciences; Energy Systems & Utilities; Human Factors &
  Ergonomics) draw on the five families in `two-layer-task-design.md` and the nearest domain below.

## Product Analytics (`product-analytics`)

### online-experimentation-a-b-test-design-readout
1. **Session metrics need the delta method.** When users are randomized but the metric is a ratio over sessions or page views, those units are correlated within user. Variance must come from the delta method or a user-level bootstrap; a session-level t-test understates the standard error and declares noise significant. Shortcut: Run a two-sample t-test treating every session as an independent observation. Pin: The design states randomization by user and defines the metric as total clicks over total sessions per arm.
2. **Peeking inflates the false positive rate.** Checking significance daily and stopping at the first p below alpha raises the real false positive rate far above alpha. The readout must use the pre-registered horizon or the named sequential boundary; the first crossing is not a valid win. Shortcut: Declare the winner on the first day the running p-value dips below 0.05. Pin: The test plan fixes a sample size or names an alpha-spending rule, and the daily results log is supplied.
3. **Guardrail safety needs non-inferiority, not silence.** A guardrail passes only if the confidence interval excludes harm beyond the stated tolerance. A non-significant guardrail with a wide interval has not shown safety; underpowered guardrails must be read as inconclusive, which can block launch. Shortcut: Treat any guardrail without a significant drop as passed. Pin: The plan states a maximum tolerable degradation per guardrail, so the interval bound, not the p-value, decides.

### observational-causal-inference
1. **Adopters were already your best users.** Users who opt into a feature were more engaged before it existed. Comparisons must condition on pre-adoption engagement trajectories, by matching or difference-in-differences with checked pre-trends; raw adopter versus non-adopter retention mostly measures selection. Shortcut: Compare retention of adopters and non-adopters after launch and call the gap the feature effect. Pin: The prompt says the feature was not randomized and supplies pre-launch activity for every user.
2. **Staggered rollouts break two-way fixed effects.** When markets or platforms get the feature at different dates and effects grow over time, two-way fixed effects uses already-treated units as controls and can flip the sign. Use group-time estimates against not-yet-treated units, then aggregate. Shortcut: Regress the outcome on a treated dummy with unit and week fixed effects. Pin: Rollout dates differ by unit and the estimand is the average effect by weeks since rollout.
3. **Ever-used windows create immortal time.** Classing users as feature users if they used it any time in their first month gives them guaranteed survival until first use. Exposure must be time-varying or fixed at a landmark date, or the feature looks protective even with no effect. Shortcut: Label users by whether they ever used the feature in the window, then compare survival from signup. Pin: The question asks for retention from signup and first-use dates vary within the classification window.

### metric-definition-measurement-science
1. **Validate metrics against known interventions.** A candidate north-star metric must be scored on past experiments with known outcomes: how often it detects real effects and agrees in direction, against its variance. The metric with the largest raw movement is often the noisiest, not the most sensitive. Shortcut: Pick the candidate that moved most in percent terms across past launches. Pin: The prompt supplies labelled past interventions and states sensitivity and directional agreement as the selection criteria.
2. **Ratio of sums is not mean of ratios.** A per-user average and a pooled ratio answer different questions; pooled ratios are dominated by heavy users. The metric must follow the stated unit of analysis, and its variance must match that choice. Shortcut: Divide total events by total users or sessions regardless of the stated unit. Pin: The metric definition names the unit, such as the average user's rate, which fixes the aggregation.
3. **Mix shift masquerades as behavior change.** A topline metric can fall while every segment improves if the user mix shifts toward low-baseline groups such as new users or a weaker platform. Decompose the change into within-segment rate and composition effects before attributing it to behavior. Shortcut: Read the topline change as a change in how users behave. Pin: The question asks whether user behavior changed, and segment sizes and rates are given for both periods.

### retention-cohort-lifetime-value-modeling
1. **Young cohorts are censored, not churned.** Cohorts acquired recently have not reached later ages. Retention at a fixed age must use only cohorts observed that long, or a survival estimator; counting unobserved months as churn or averaging unequal horizons biases the curve. Shortcut: Average month-N retention over all cohorts, filling unobserved ages as zero or dropping them silently. Pin: Cohort acquisition dates and the data cutoff are given and the prompt asks retention at a fixed age.
2. **Constant churn understates heterogeneous lifetime.** Aggregate churn falls with tenure because fragile users leave first. Extrapolating lifetime value from early churn as a constant rate understates value; a heterogeneity model such as shifted-beta-geometric fits the flattening curve. Shortcut: Take the first-period churn rate and compute lifetime as one over that rate. Pin: The prompt names a heterogeneity-aware model or the observed curve visibly flattens over the stated horizon.
3. **Resample users, not cohort cells.** Lifetime value uncertainty must reflect user-level sampling within each cohort, with every period of a user kept together. Treating monthly retention rates as independent ignores their dependence and small cohort sizes, giving bounds far too narrow. Shortcut: Compute intervals from the spread of monthly cohort retention rates as if independent. Pin: The prompt asks for uncertainty bounds by cohort and user-level histories are supplied.

### churn-propensity-uplift-modeling
1. **Target persuadables, not likely retainers.** Uplift is the treatment effect for a user, not their retention probability. Models must be trained and evaluated on randomized treatment and control, ranked by Qini or uplift curves; high-propensity users often retain anyway. Shortcut: Send the promotion to users with the highest predicted retention probability. Pin: The prompt asks whose retention would most increase and supplies a randomized promotion holdout.
2. **Features must predate the prediction moment.** Churn features computed through the label window leak the outcome, such as declining activity during the churn month. Build features as of a fixed cutoff, label from the period after, and backtest on a later cutoff. Shortcut: Compute features over the full history and label churn on the same extract. Pin: The prompt fixes a scoring date and churn definition, so only pre-cutoff information is admissible.
3. **Score uplift with Qini, not outcome accuracy.** Individual effects are never observed, so uplift models must be evaluated on randomized holdout data with Qini or uplift curves comparing treated and control response by score bin. The model with the best conversion AUC can rank incremental response poorly. Shortcut: Pick the model with the highest AUC for predicting conversion among treated customers. Pin: The question asks which model best targets incremental response and supplies a randomized treatment and control sample.
4. **Rank by effect, not treated response.** Customers most likely to buy when contacted include sure things who buy anyway. Targeting must rank by the estimated difference between treated and untreated response; ranking by treated purchase probability spends budget on sure things and can include customers harmed by contact. Shortcut: Target the top decile of customers by predicted purchase probability under treatment. Pin: The objective is stated as incremental conversions or incremental profit per contact, which requires the treated minus control difference.
5. **Cut where marginal uplift value meets cost.** Targeting depth is set where the next segment's incremental profit, estimated uplift times margin minus contact cost, turns negative. Maximizing total incremental conversions or Qini area picks a deeper cut that adds conversions but loses money. Shortcut: Target every segment with positive estimated uplift, or the depth that maximizes total incremental conversions. Pin: The task states a margin per conversion and a cost per contact and asks for the profit-maximizing targeting share.
6. **Recalibrate scores trained on rebalanced samples.** When churners were oversampled or non-churners downsampled for training, raw scores overstate churn probability. Expected-value targeting needs probabilities corrected back to the deployment base rate before multiplying by customer value and comparing with offer cost. Shortcut: Plug the raw model score from the balanced training set straight into probability times value minus cost. Pin: The prompt states the training sampling ratio and the true churn rate, and asks for expected net value or a cost-based threshold.

### funnel-journey-analysis
1. **Funnel steps must be ordered and windowed.** A funnel counts users who complete each step after the prior step within the stated window. Counting unique users per step independently mixes journeys and can show later steps exceeding earlier ones, misplacing the biggest drop-off. Shortcut: Count distinct users who fired each event in the period and divide adjacent counts. Pin: The funnel definition states the step order and a conversion window per user or session.
2. **Small segments look like the worst leaks.** Ranking segments by raw step conversion surfaces tiny segments whose extreme rates are noise. Use intervals or partial pooling such as beta-binomial shrinkage, and rank by expected lost conversions, not raw rate. Shortcut: List the segments with the lowest observed conversion as the biggest problems. Pin: The prompt asks for drop-offs with uncertainty by segment and segment sizes vary widely.
3. **Recent cohorts have not finished converting.** Users who entered the funnel recently have not yet had the full conversion window. Period comparisons must use only cohorts whose window has fully elapsed; counting late entrants as non-converters makes the latest period look like a conversion drop. Shortcut: Compare this week's entrants to last month's using conversions observed so far and report the decline. Pin: The task fixes a conversion window per cohort and asks whether conversion changed, with the data extract ending mid-window.

### network-effect-marketplace-experimentation
1. **Shared supply contaminates user-level tests.** In a two-sided marketplace, treated buyers draw down inventory or seller attention that control buyers would have used, inflating the measured lift. Randomize and analyze at the cluster or time-slot level that contains the interference. Shortcut: Randomize individual buyers or sellers and compare arm means. Pin: The design names cluster or switchback randomization because both sides share inventory within a market.
2. **Switchbacks need burn-in and period-level inference.** After each switch, the previous regime carries over for a while, and adjacent periods are correlated. Drop the stated burn-in, analyze at the period level, and use randomization inference over the switching schedule. Shortcut: Pool all orders in treated periods versus control periods and run an order-level test. Pin: The switch interval, carryover window and randomized schedule are stated in the design.
3. **Weight by exposure probability under network interference.** With network interference, a user's outcome depends on how many friends are treated. Estimate the global effect by comparing units in specified exposure conditions, weighted by each unit's probability of that exposure; raw treated versus control compares partially exposed groups and understates the effect. Shortcut: Compare mean outcomes of treated and control users as if each user's outcome depended only on their own assignment. Pin: The estimand is the effect of treating everyone versus no one, and the design supplies the friendship graph and assignment probabilities.

### feature-adoption-diffusion-modeling
1. **Separate novelty from durable usage.** Early usage of a new feature includes curiosity trials that fade. Estimate effects by time since first exposure and judge durability from the later plateau or repeat use, not the average over the whole window. Shortcut: Average daily usage across the whole post-launch period and call it the steady state. Pin: The prompt asks for durable usage, and per-user exposure dates span enough weeks to see decay.
2. **Adoption rates need the eligible denominator.** During a staged rollout, only exposed and eligible users can adopt. Adoption hazards and diffusion curves must use the at-risk eligible base each period; dividing by all users confounds adoption speed with rollout speed. Shortcut: Divide cumulative adopters by total active users and fit a curve to that share. Pin: Rollout eligibility dates per user are given and the prompt asks how fast eligible users adopt.
3. **Censored non-adopters bias time to adoption.** Users not yet adopting by the data cutoff are right-censored, not failures. Time to adoption must come from a survival estimator such as Kaplan-Meier on the eligible cohort; averaging only observed adopters' times understates delay and inflates apparent speed. Shortcut: Compute median days from eligibility to first use among users who adopted, dropping everyone who has not adopted yet. Pin: The prompt asks for median time to adoption or adoption by day N, and many eligible users remain unadopted at extract end.

## Marketing & Consumer Research (`marketing-consumer-research`)

### segmentation-market-structure
1. **Clusters always appear; stability must be shown.** Any clustering algorithm returns k groups even in structureless data. The segment count must be chosen by a stated stability check (bootstrap or split-half agreement), so a solution that looks crisp once but reshuffles under resampling is rejected. Shortcut: Run k-means once, pick k from the elbow plot, and report the segments as real. Pin: The question asks for stable segments and names the resampling agreement measure and threshold a solution must pass.
2. **Size segments with posterior probabilities, not modal assignment.** In a latent class model each respondent has a probability of belonging to each class. Segment sizes and their uncertainty come from summing posterior probabilities and propagating parameter uncertainty; hard assignment to the most likely class biases sizes toward the larger classes. Shortcut: Assign everyone to their highest-probability class, count heads, and quote a binomial interval. Pin: The model is a latent class model and the prompt asks for segment sizes with intervals that reflect classification uncertainty.
3. **Scale-use style masquerades as attitude segments.** Respondents differ in how they use rating scales. Clustering raw agreement scores splits people into high raters and low raters rather than distinct attitudes, so ratings must be centred within respondent, or a forced-choice measure used, before clustering. Shortcut: Cluster the raw 1 to 7 agreement ratings directly. Pin: The stated procedure requires within-respondent standardisation of attitude batteries, or the battery is a best-worst design that removes scale use.
4. **Switching must be judged against share-based expectation.** Raw brand-switching counts mostly reflect brand size. Market partitions come from comparing observed duplication with the expectation under the duplication-of-purchase law; only excess duplication signals a closer competitive submarket. Shortcut: Call the brands with the most switchers between them the closest competitors. Pin: The prompt defines a submarket as pairs whose duplication exceeds the share-proportional expectation by a stated margin.

### conjoint-choice-willingness-to-pay-analysis
1. **Logit simulators steal share from everyone equally.** Aggregate logit share simulation has the independence of irrelevant alternatives property: a near-clone takes share proportionally from all products. Simulations must use individual-level draws or a stated randomized first choice rule so a clone cannibalises mainly its twin. Shortcut: Plug aggregate part-worths into a single logit share formula and read off the new product's share. Pin: The task supplies individual-level posterior draws and names the simulation rule, and the scenario adds a product nearly identical to an existing one.
2. **WTP is a ratio of random utilities.** WTP equals an attribute utility divided by the price coefficient. Computing it per respondent draw and summarising with the stated statistic differs from dividing average utilities, and near-zero price coefficients make the mean explode, which is why the median is usually specified. Shortcut: Divide the mean attribute part-worth by the mean price coefficient. Pin: The prompt defines WTP as the population median of individual-level WTP computed across posterior draws.
3. **Preference share is not market share.** Shares among the tested profiles ignore buyers who would choose nothing. Volume forecasts must include the no-choice alternative as estimated, and only then apply any stated calibration to market data; dropping it inflates demand for every product. Shortcut: Report each product's share among the products shown as its expected market share. Pin: The design included a no-choice or dual-response option and the question asks for expected purchase volume, not relative preference.
4. **Do not simulate prices outside the tested range.** Part-worths are only identified at tested levels. Prices between levels are interpolated as the analysis plan states; a linear price coefficient extrapolated beyond the range invents a demand curve the experiment never measured, so out-of-range scenarios are reported as unsupported. Shortcut: Fit a linear price term and simulate any price management asks about. Pin: The prompt fixes the tested price levels, specifies piecewise-linear interpolation, and asks which in-range price maximises the stated objective.

### brand-campaign-ad-effectiveness-measurement
1. **Exposed versus unexposed is targeting, not lift.** Ad platforms show ads to people already likely to buy. Incremental lift must come from the randomized assignment contrast (intent to treat), divided by the exposure rate for the exposed, not from comparing people who saw the ad with those who did not. Shortcut: Compare conversion of exposed users with unexposed users and call the gap lift. Pin: The campaign had a randomized holdout and the question asks for lift caused by the campaign among exposed users.
2. **Compare exposed with would-have-been-exposed controls.** Exposed treated users differ from the whole control group, because only some users ever reach an ad slot. The valid comparison is with control users flagged as would-have-been-exposed (ghost ads) or an ITT divided by exposure rate. Shortcut: Compare exposed users in the test group with every user in the control group. Pin: The data carries a ghost-impression flag in the control arm and the estimand is the effect on users who were exposed.
3. **Carryover and saturation set marginal return.** Advertising works with delay and diminishing returns. A mix model must transform spend with the stated adstock decay and saturation curve; marginal return on the next dollar is the curve's slope at current spend, not the average return over all spend. Shortcut: Regress weekly sales on same-week spend and quote the coefficient as return per dollar. Pin: The prompt specifies the adstock and saturation forms and asks for marginal return at a stated spend increase.
4. **Geo tests have markets as the unit.** When regions are randomized, the independent units are the regions. Lift and its interval come from a market-level comparison using pre-period data (difference in differences or a synthetic control), not from millions of users treated as independent. Shortcut: Pool all users in test and control regions and run a user-level t-test. Pin: Assignment was by region, the number of regions is small, and the question asks whether lift is significant at a stated level.

### customer-satisfaction-driver-analysis
1. **Correlated drivers need variance decomposition.** Service attributes are highly correlated, so multiple regression coefficients flip sign and size with small data changes. Relative importance must be shared out with the stated method, such as Shapley or relative weights, which split explained variance and sum to the total. Shortcut: Rank drivers by their standardized regression coefficients. Pin: The prompt asks for each attribute's share of explained variance in overall satisfaction using a named decomposition.
2. **Net Promoter change needs multinomial error.** NPS is the difference of two proportions from one multinomial sample, so its variance includes their covariance. Wave-to-wave changes must be tested with that variance; treating NPS as a mean of plus and minus scores mishandles it or ignores sampling noise entirely. Shortcut: Declare any NPS movement of a few points between waves a real change. Pin: The question asks whether the score changed between independent waves at a stated significance level.
3. **Penalties and rewards are asymmetric.** Some attributes hurt satisfaction when poor but add little when excellent, others the reverse. Penalty-reward analysis estimates the drop below adequate and the gain above it separately; one linear coefficient averages them and misclassifies basic needs as unimportant. Shortcut: Fit one linear slope per attribute and rank by it. Pin: The prompt asks for separate penalty and reward effects relative to a stated performance threshold.
4. **Link satisfaction to later loyalty behavior.** Stated intent to return is measured with the same halo as satisfaction. Loyalty effects must use subsequent observed behavior in a window after the survey, among all surveyed customers, so the predictor precedes the outcome and is not tautological. Shortcut: Correlate satisfaction with stated likelihood to repurchase from the same questionnaire. Pin: The prompt defines loyalty as renewal or repeat purchase observed within a stated period after the survey date.

### media-reach-frequency-audience-measurement
1. **Channel reaches overlap; do not add them.** Combined reach counts unique people. Duplication must come from single-source panel data, where people exposed through several channels are counted once; summing channel reaches overstates, and the random-duplication formula misses the real correlation between heavy users. Shortcut: Add television, web and mobile reach together, or apply independence across channels. Pin: A single-source panel records each person's exposures across channels and the question asks for unduplicated reach.
2. **Average frequency hides who reaches threshold.** Effective reach at three or more exposures needs the frequency distribution, which is skewed: heavy viewers get many exposures and light viewers few. GRPs divided by reach gives an average that, applied to everyone, overstates effective reach. Shortcut: Compute average frequency and count everyone as reached at that frequency. Pin: The prompt defines effective reach at a stated minimum frequency and the panel supplies person-level exposure counts or a fitted distribution.
3. **Campaign reach is not summed weekly reach.** Reach accumulates at a decreasing rate because the same people are reached week after week. Campaign reach must be the share of persons with at least one exposure over the whole flight, so weekly figures cannot be added or averaged. Shortcut: Sum the weekly reach figures across the flight. Pin: The question asks for cumulative unique reach across the full campaign window with person-level data supplied.
4. **Project panel results with person weights.** Measurement panels over- and under-represent groups that differ in media use. Reach and frequency must be computed with each panelist's weight projected to the stated universe; unweighted panel proportions misstate reach whenever heavy users are over-represented. Shortcut: Report the unweighted share of panelists reached as population reach. Pin: The panel file supplies projection weights and a universe estimate, and the question asks for reach in that universe.

## Quantitative Finance (`quantitative-finance`)

### alpha-research (taxonomy task type: strategy-backtesting-evaluation, factor-return-attribution)
1. **Fama-MacBeth errors, not pooled OLS.** A factor premium is the time-series mean of monthly cross-sectional slopes, tested with its time-series standard error (Newey-West if autocorrelated). Pooled OLS treats every firm-month as independent, so cross-sectional correlation shrinks the standard error and manufactures significance. Shortcut: Run one pooled regression of returns on characteristics and read the OLS t-statistic. Pin: The prompt asks whether a premium is priced in the cross-section, which is the Fama-MacBeth estimand with time-series inference.
2. **Lag accounting data to when it existed.** Book equity and other fundamentals must be matched to returns only after they were publicly available, using the standard reporting lag. Aligning fiscal year-end values to the next month's return uses information nobody had and inflates the value premium. Shortcut: Merge fundamentals to returns on fiscal period end date. Pin: A stated portfolio formation convention (for example annual rebalance with a fixed reporting lag) fixes which accounting vintage each return may use.
3. **Deflate the best signal for trials.** When many signals or parameter sets were tried, the best backtest Sharpe is inflated by selection. Significance must account for the number of trials, using a stated haircut, deflated Sharpe ratio or multiple-testing hurdle. Shortcut: Report the winning signal's t-statistic as if it were the only one tested. Pin: The task discloses the number of variants searched and the hurdle to apply.

### backtesting (taxonomy task type: strategy-backtesting-evaluation)
1. **Trade on the next bar.** A signal computed from a bar's close can only be traded after that close, with costs on the turnover it implies. Filling at the same close the signal used, or ignoring costs, books returns that were never available. Shortcut: Apply the signal to the same period's return and ignore transaction costs. Pin: The backtest spec states execution timing and a cost model, leaving one realisable return series.
2. **Use the point-in-time universe.** A backtest must draw each date's tradable set from constituents as of that date, including later delisted names and their delisting returns. Using today's survivors removes failures and inflates historical performance. Shortcut: Backtest over the current index members for the whole history. Pin: The prompt defines the universe by historical membership rules, so survivors-only is outside the stated design.
3. **Link multi-period attribution, do not sum.** Single-period Brinson allocation and selection effects compound, so summing monthly effects does not reproduce cumulative active return and can misstate which effect dominated. The analysis must apply a stated linking method, such as Carino or Menchero, so effects add exactly to geometric excess return. Shortcut: Add up monthly allocation and selection effects and report the leftover gap as interaction or noise. Pin: The question asks for full-period attribution that reconciles to compounded active return, under a named linking convention.

### derivative-pricing (taxonomy task type: volatility-derivatives-pricing)
1. **Price exotics with the calibrated model.** A barrier price depends on smile dynamics, not only today's smile. Once a stochastic-volatility model is calibrated, the barrier must be priced in that model; plugging one Black-Scholes implied vol into a closed form ignores skew and vol-of-vol. Shortcut: Price the barrier with Black-Scholes at the strike's implied volatility. Pin: The prompt names the model to calibrate and asks for the barrier price and greeks under it.
2. **Honour the barrier monitoring frequency.** A barrier checked at discrete fixings is breached less often than a continuously monitored one, so knock-out values are higher. Discrete monitoring needs simulation on the fixing dates or a continuity correction of the barrier level. Shortcut: Use the continuous-monitoring closed form for a daily-fixed barrier. Pin: The term sheet states the monitoring dates, so only a price on those dates answers the question.
3. **Value early exercise by backward induction.** An American option is worth its continuation value or exercise value, whichever is larger, at every exercise date. That needs backward induction on a tree or PDE grid, or least squares Monte Carlo with an out-of-sample exercise rule. European formulas omit the early exercise premium. Shortcut: Price the American option with the European closed form, or exercise whenever intrinsic value exceeds today's European price. Pin: The contract grants early exercise on stated dates and the prompt names the pricing model, so only the optimal-stopping value answers.

### risk-modeling (taxonomy task type: portfolio-construction-risk)
1. **Test exception counts and their clustering.** A VaR backtest compares the exception count to a binomial at the stated level (Kupiec) and checks exceptions are independent (Christoffersen). A model can pass on count while exceptions bunch in a stress period, which the conditional test rejects. Shortcut: Count exceptions, see the number is near expected, declare the model fine. Pin: The prompt asks to backtest exceptions, and a named test or traffic-light standard defines the rejection rule and confidence level.
2. **Expected Shortfall averages the whole tail.** ES is the mean loss beyond the VaR quantile, so it depends on every tail observation and on the estimator's tail-count convention. Reporting a higher-confidence VaR, or a normal-scaled ES on fat-tailed P&L, understates the tail. Shortcut: Use VaR at a higher confidence, or multiply volatility by the normal ES factor. Pin: The stated method (historical simulation, window length, confidence level) makes the tail set and its average a single number.
3. **Exposure is per netting set, collateral lags.** Counterparty exposure must be simulated per legally enforceable netting set, with collateral applied only after the margin period of risk. Summing positive exposures trade by trade, or treating collateral as instantly posted, misstates expected exposure and can reverse which counterparty consumes most capital. Shortcut: Add up each trade's positive mark-to-market and subtract the current collateral balance. Pin: The task supplies netting agreements and a collateral schedule and asks for expected positive exposure under the stated margin period.

### portfolio-construction-optimization (taxonomy task type: portfolio-construction-risk)
1. **Solve the constrained problem, do not clip.** Long-only and max-weight limits change the optimum: binding constraints redistribute weight among the remaining assets via the quadratic program. Clipping unconstrained weights and renormalising gives a feasible but suboptimal portfolio with a different allocation. Shortcut: Compute unconstrained mean-variance weights, zero the negatives, cap at the limit, rescale to one. Pin: The prompt states the objective and constraints explicitly, so the unique QP solution is the answer.
2. **Measure sensitivity by re-optimising perturbed inputs.** Optimized weights are highly sensitive to expected-return error. Sensitivity must be assessed by resampling or perturbing inputs within their estimation error and re-solving, and comparing weights and out-of-sample performance, not by quoting the in-sample frontier, which is optimistically biased. Shortcut: Report the in-sample Sharpe and frontier of the optimal portfolio as its robustness. Pin: A stated resampling procedure (sample length, draws, seed) makes the dispersion of re-optimized weights reproducible.
3. **Shrink the covariance before inverting it.** With many assets relative to observations, the sample covariance is ill-conditioned and its inverse amplifies estimation noise into extreme weights. The rigorous build uses the stated shrinkage or factor estimator; the raw sample matrix yields different weights and understates out-of-sample risk. Shortcut: Plug the sample covariance matrix of historical returns directly into the optimizer. Pin: The mandate names a shrinkage or factor covariance estimator, or judges portfolios on out-of-sample risk with few observations per asset.

### fixed-income-term-structure-analysis (taxonomy task type: volatility-derivatives-pricing, portfolio-construction-risk)
1. **Par rates are not zero rates.** Zero rates come from bootstrapping sequentially: each par instrument's earlier cash flows are discounted on already-solved factors, with its own day count, frequency and interpolation on a stated quantity. Treating par yields as zero rates misprices every coupon bond off the curve. Shortcut: Interpolate quoted swap and bond yields and use them directly as discount rates. Pin: Instrument conventions and the stated interpolation scheme determine one bootstrapped curve.
2. **Bump the quotes, then rebuild.** DV01 and key-rate durations defined on market quotes require shifting each input quote, rebuilding the curve and repricing. Shifting zero rates directly, or using a modified-duration approximation, gives different sensitivities and key rates that need not sum to the parallel figure. Shortcut: Use modified duration times price, or shift the zero curve by one basis point. Pin: The prompt or risk standard defines the bump (which quotes, size, tenors), fixing the repricing procedure.
3. **Hedge curve twists with key-rate durations.** A hedge sized to match total modified duration only neutralizes parallel shifts. Under a steepening or twist the residual exposure can be large; hedge ratios must solve for matching partial durations at each key tenor using the available instruments. Shortcut: Size a single hedge so portfolio modified duration equals zero and call the position rate neutral. Pin: The question specifies a non-parallel scenario or asks to neutralize exposure at named tenors with several hedge instruments.

### credit-default-risk-modeling (taxonomy task type: portfolio-construction-risk; banking: credit-scoring-pd-modeling)
1. **Handle withdrawn ratings explicitly.** In a rating-transition matrix, obligors whose ratings are withdrawn leave the sample before their outcome is known. Counting them as surviving understates default rates; the standard removes them or redistributes them proportionally, and cohort versus duration methods differ. Shortcut: Treat withdrawn obligors as not defaulted in the one-year cohort. Pin: A stated estimation convention (cohort method, withdrawal adjustment) makes the transition probabilities and implied PDs unique.
2. **Portfolio tail loss needs default correlation.** Defaults cluster because obligors share systematic risk, so a high-quantile portfolio loss must come from a correlated model such as a one-factor Gaussian copula. Assuming independent defaults collapses the loss distribution around expected loss and badly understates the tail. Shortcut: Simulate or compute portfolio losses treating each obligor's default as independent given its PD. Pin: The task asks for a 99.9% loss quantile or economic capital and states an asset correlation or factor loading.
3. **Structural PD needs asset value and volatility.** In a Merton-type model, distance to default uses unobserved asset value and asset volatility, solved jointly from equity value, equity volatility and debt. Plugging equity volatility in as asset volatility, or market cap as assets, misstates leverage and the PD. Shortcut: Use market capitalization as asset value and equity volatility directly in the distance-to-default formula. Pin: The task names the Merton or KMV structural model and supplies equity prices, debt barrier and risk-free rate, not asset values.

### liquidity-funding-balance-sheet-risk (taxonomy task type: banking: deposit-liquidity-behaviour)
1. **Contingent outflows scale with the stress.** Committed credit lines, derivative margin calls and downgrade collateral triggers draw cash exactly when the stress hits. Rigorous analysis revalues these contingent outflows under the scenario's market and rating shocks; normal-period drawdown and margin history understates stressed outflows and survival risk. Shortcut: Project contingent outflows from average historical line drawdowns and margin calls observed in normal periods. Pin: The scenario states market moves and a rating downgrade, and line commitments and collateral agreement terms are supplied.
2. **Survival horizon is the first cumulative breach.** Liquidity survival depends on the running cumulative gap across maturity buckets. The rigorous analysis tracks cumulative net cash flow, plus counterbalancing capacity available by each date, and finds the first breach; a full-horizon net position can be positive while an early bucket runs dry. Shortcut: Net total stressed inflows against outflows over the whole horizon and declare the bank liquid if positive. Pin: The question asks for the survival horizon or whether the bank survives every day of a stated stress window.
3. **Behavioral maturity, not contractual, for sticky deposits.** Non-maturity deposits are contractually overnight but largely sticky. Funding gap and stable-funding analysis must split them into stable core and volatile runoff using the stated behavioral model; contractual maturity puts all deposits in the first bucket and shows a crisis that is not there. Shortcut: Slot all demand and savings deposits in the overnight bucket at their contractual maturity. Pin: The question asks for the going-concern or behavioral liquidity gap and supplies deposit decay or core-balance estimates.

### scenario-analysis-enterprise-stress-testing (taxonomy task type: banking: stress-testing-capital)
1. **Stress correlations are not calm correlations.** Joint losses in a stress scenario depend on how risk drivers move together under stress, and correlations rise in crises. Aggregating losses with full-sample or calm-period covariance understates the tail; estimate dependence on the stress regime or condition on the scenario. Shortcut: Combine driver losses using a correlation matrix estimated on the full calm-dominated history. Pin: The history spans a crisis period and the question asks for portfolio loss under a stated stressed regime.
2. **Decompose losses so the parts sum.** Shocking one driver at a time ignores interactions, so the pieces do not add to the joint-scenario loss. A rigorous attribution uses the stated scheme (sequential order or symmetric averaging over orders) and reconciles exactly to the total. Shortcut: Run each driver alone and present the standalone losses as the decomposition. Pin: The prompt asks to decompose the scenario result, and a stated attribution order or averaging rule makes it unique.
3. **Large shocks need full revaluation.** Option, prepayment and callable positions are nonlinear, so delta or duration sensitivities misstate losses under severe moves, often missing convexity sign flips. Stress losses must come from repricing each position at the shocked factors; linear sensitivity sums diverge most exactly where stress matters. Shortcut: Multiply each position's delta or duration by the scenario shock and sum the products as the stressed loss. Pin: Shocks far exceed the sensitivities' local range, pricing inputs are supplied, and the stated standard requires full revaluation.

### market-data-curve-surface-validation (taxonomy task type: volatility-derivatives-pricing)
1. **Test arbitrage on total variance and prices.** Calendar arbitrage means total implied variance falls with maturity at fixed moneyness; butterfly arbitrage means call prices are not convex in strike. Falling implied vol across maturities, or a dip in one smile, is often arbitrage-free. Shortcut: Flag any implied vol that decreases with maturity or is non-monotone in strike. Pin: The no-arbitrage conditions are mathematical, so the set of violating quotes is fully determined.
2. **Validate a curve by repricing its inputs.** A bootstrapped curve is valid when it reprices every input instrument to par under that instrument's day count, compounding and settlement lag. Comparing zero rates directly with quoted par yields flags spurious errors at long tenors, where coupon effects legitimately separate the two. Shortcut: Flag tenors where the zero rate differs from the quoted par yield by more than the tolerance. Pin: The standard defines a pass as repricing error per input instrument within tolerance, with conventions listed per instrument.
3. **Inspect implied forwards, not the zero curve.** Linear interpolation on zero rates gives a smooth-looking zero curve whose implied forwards jump at every knot. Validation must derive forwards on the stated grid and test them against the smoothness or positivity criterion; eyeballing zeros passes a curve that misprices forward-starting products. Shortcut: Plot zero rates, see a smooth monotone curve, and approve it. Pin: The acceptance criterion is stated on forward rates, such as a maximum jump between adjacent one-month forwards or non-negativity.

### counterparty-exposure-valuation-adjustment (taxonomy task type: portfolio-construction-risk, volatility-derivatives-pricing)
1. **Net within netting sets, collateralise with lag.** Exposure is the positive part of the netted portfolio value per netting set, reduced by collateral that lags by the margin period of risk. Summing positive trade-level values overstates exposure; ignoring the margin period understates it. Shortcut: Simulate each trade, floor at zero, and add trades up by counterparty. Pin: The supplied netting and collateral agreements (thresholds, minimum transfer, margin period) fix the exposure profile.
2. **CVA uses market-implied default and discounting.** CVA integrates discounted expected exposure against marginal default probabilities from market credit spreads, times loss given default. Historical rating PDs or undiscounted exposure answer a capital question, not the fair-value adjustment. Shortcut: Multiply average exposure by a historical one-year PD and a fixed recovery. Pin: The prompt asks for a valuation adjustment, which requires risk-neutral spreads and the stated discount curve.
3. **Simulate wrong-way risk jointly with default.** When exposure tends to rise as the counterparty's credit worsens, CVA must use exposure conditional on default, simulated jointly with correlated credit and market drivers. Multiplying an independently computed exposure profile by default probabilities treats them as independent and understates CVA. Shortcut: Compute expected exposure and default probabilities separately, then multiply them bucket by bucket. Pin: The task states a dependence between the counterparty's credit and the trade's risk driver and asks for CVA including it.

## Banking, Credit & Financial Risk (`banking-credit-financial-risk`)

### credit-scoring-underwriting (taxonomy task type: credit-scoring-pd-modeling)
1. **Score applicants, not just booked loans.** Approval-rate and fairness limits apply to the through-the-door applicant pool. A scorecard built and evaluated only on booked accounts sees a truncated population, so its cutoff, approval rate and bad rate shift once rejects are scored and inferred. Shortcut: Fit and validate on approved loans with outcomes and set the cutoff from that sample's score distribution. Pin: The prompt states the approval-rate target over all applicants and supplies rejected applications with scores or bureau outcomes.
2. **Undo bad oversampling before setting cutoffs.** Development samples often oversample defaults. Predicted PDs from such a model must be prior-corrected to the population default rate before cutoffs are tied to expected bad rate or loss, otherwise every cutoff looks far riskier than it is. Shortcut: Read the model's raw predicted probabilities as real PDs and pick the cutoff where predicted bad rate hits target. Pin: The design documents the sampling rates for goods and bads and the task sets a cutoff by expected portfolio bad rate.
3. **Validate on a later vintage.** A scorecard is deployed on future applicants, so discrimination and calibration must be tested out of time on later vintages. A random holdout mixes vintages, hides population and macro drift, and overstates Gini and calibration. Shortcut: Randomly split all accounts into train and test and report the test Gini as expected performance. Pin: The prompt asks how the scorecard will perform on new applications, and the data spans several origination vintages.
4. **Measure fairness at the operating cutoff.** Disparity in approvals, such as an adverse impact ratio, depends on where the cutoff sits. Fix the cutoff that meets the approval-rate constraint first, then compute group approval rates; a score-level comparison can pass while the cutoff fails. Shortcut: Compare average scores or AUC across groups and declare the model fair if they look similar. Pin: The stated fairness standard is defined on approval outcomes at the chosen cutoff, alongside a stated approval-rate target.

### expected-credit-loss-allowance-modeling (taxonomy task type: expected-credit-loss-provisioning)
1. **Lifetime loss sums marginal default probabilities.** Lifetime ECL sums, period by period, the marginal probability of defaulting in that period given survival and no prepayment, times LGD and EAD, discounted at the effective rate. Scaling a 12-month PD by remaining term ignores survival and double counts. Shortcut: Multiply the annual PD by remaining years and by current balance and LGD. Pin: The prompt invokes the CECL or IFRS 9 lifetime definition with a contractual life and a term structure of PDs.
2. **Weight the losses, not the scenarios.** Credit losses are convex in macro conditions, so the allowance is the probability-weighted average of ECL computed under each scenario. Running the model once on a probability-weighted average scenario understates losses because the stress scenario's tail is averaged away. Shortcut: Blend scenario macro paths by their weights into one path and compute ECL once. Pin: The standard requires an unbiased probability-weighted estimate and the task gives scenario weights and macro paths.
3. **Allowance PDs must be point-in-time.** Allowance PDs must reflect current and forecast conditions. Long-run average default rates by grade describe the cycle average, so in a downturn or recovery they misstate expected losses; conditioning PDs on macro drivers through a fitted link aligns them with conditions. Shortcut: Use the long-run average default rate per rating grade as the twelve-month PD. Pin: Historical default rates come with macro series, and the prompt asks for a forward-looking allowance under current conditions.
4. **Open workouts bias LGD downward.** Defaults still in workout are excluded from realized LGD, yet long, costly workouts are the ones still open, so resolved-only LGD understates loss. Model recovery by time since default or treat open cases as censored, discounting recoveries net of costs. Shortcut: Average LGD over closed workouts only, using undiscounted recoveries. Pin: The task asks for downturn or lifetime LGD and the data includes recovery cash flows and status for open defaults.

### fraud-aml-transaction-monitoring (taxonomy task type: fraud-aml-detection)
1. **Set thresholds by reviewer capacity yield.** When the goal is true investigations per reviewer hour under fixed capacity, the threshold is chosen from alert precision and review time at each score band until hours run out. AUC or F1 optimum ignores capacity and review cost. Shortcut: Pick the threshold that maximises F1 or sits at the ROC curve's best point. Pin: The prompt states reviewer hours, handling time per alert, and the per-hour productivity metric as the objective.
2. **Labels exist only above the line.** Only alerted cases get investigated, so precision below the current threshold is unobserved. Estimating the effect of lowering the threshold requires the below-the-line random sample, weighted back to its stratum; extrapolating from investigated alerts is selection bias. Shortcut: Treat uninvestigated transactions as confirmed legitimate when estimating precision at a lower threshold. Pin: The design includes a below-the-line sample with its sampling rate and the task asks about moving the threshold.
3. **Split by entity, not by transaction.** Suspicious activity is filed per customer or network, and transactions from one network share features. Train and test must be split by entity and evaluated at case level, or the model memorises networks and alert metrics look far better than in production. Shortcut: Randomly split transactions and report transaction-level precision and recall. Pin: The prompt measures productivity in investigations or cases, and transactions carry customer and counterparty identifiers.
4. **Aggregate over linked accounts and windows.** Structuring splits value across accounts, days and counterparties so each transaction stays under a threshold. Detection must aggregate over the transaction network and a rolling window; per-transaction rules or per-account scores miss the pattern entirely. Shortcut: Flag individual transactions above an amount threshold or score each account in isolation. Pin: The task asks to detect anomalous networks and supplies sender, receiver and timestamp links with a stated aggregation window.

### deposit-prepayment-interest-rate-behavior (taxonomy task type: deposit-liquidity-behaviour)
1. **Deposit beta is cumulative cycle pass-through.** Deposit beta for ALM is the cumulative change in the deposit rate divided by the cumulative change in the policy rate over a cycle, allowing for lagged repricing. Contemporaneous monthly changes understate it, and regressing rate levels gives a spurious fit. Shortcut: Regress monthly deposit rate changes on same-month policy rate changes and call the slope the beta. Pin: The prompt defines beta as cycle-to-date pass-through for a stated rate cycle used in the ALM simulation.
2. **Betas differ for rising and falling rates.** Banks pass rate rises to depositors slowly and cuts quickly, so deposit betas are asymmetric by direction and often grow as rates rise. One pooled beta misprices both the up and down shocks in an asset-liability run. Shortcut: Estimate one beta over the whole sample and apply it symmetrically to all rate shocks. Pin: The simulation requires both rate-up and rate-down scenarios and the history contains complete hiking and easing cycles.
3. **Estimate deposit decay on cohorts, not balances.** Total non-maturity balances grow as new accounts open, masking attrition of the existing book. Runoff for asset-liability use must be estimated on vintage cohorts of accounts open at the start date, tracking their surviving balances over time. Shortcut: Fit a decay curve to the total portfolio balance series and apply it to the current book. Pin: Data carries account opening dates and the question asks for the runoff profile of balances existing today.
4. **Burnout lowers speeds for exposed pools.** Prepayment depends on refinance incentive and on history: pools that already faced strong incentives have lost their most rate-sensitive borrowers. One incentive S-curve fit across all pools overprojects speeds for seasoned, previously in-the-money pools. Shortcut: Fit one prepayment curve on rate incentive pooled across all vintages and apply it to every pool. Pin: The data includes pools with different incentive histories and the prompt asks for projections on seasoned pools.

### collections-loss-mitigation-strategy (taxonomy task type: collections-recovery)
1. **Compare by assignment, not treatment received.** When contact or hardship offers are randomly assigned, incremental recovery is the difference by assigned arm. Comparing customers actually reached or enrolled with the rest confounds treatment with willingness to engage; non-compliance calls for intention to treat or an instrumented complier effect. Shortcut: Compare recoveries of customers who were reached or accepted the offer against those who were not. Pin: The design records random assignment and the prompt asks for the policy's incremental recovery.
2. **Count net, discounted recovery over one horizon.** Incremental recovery is cash collected net of treatment cost and discounted, over the same horizon for every arm. Counting early cures as wins ignores re-default and the slower cash of hardship plans, which can reverse the ranking of treatments. Shortcut: Rank treatments by cure rate or payment made within the first month. Pin: The prompt states the evaluation horizon, discount rate and treatment costs and defines recovery as net cash.
3. **Use the eligibility cutoff as a discontinuity.** Where hardship programs are offered by a rule on delinquency or score, the credible quasi-experimental estimate is a local comparison at the cutoff with an appropriate bandwidth. Enrolled versus non-enrolled across the whole book confounds treatment with distress. Shortcut: Regress recovery on an enrollment dummy with a few controls over the full portfolio. Pin: The eligibility rule is a stated threshold on a running variable that borrowers cannot precisely manipulate.
4. **Target by uplift, not likelihood to pay.** Scarce treatments should go where the treatment effect is largest. Accounts most likely to pay often self-cure regardless, so ranking by predicted repayment spends effort on sure things; allocation must use heterogeneous treatment effects estimated from the experiment. Shortcut: Assign the costly treatment to accounts with the highest predicted probability of repayment. Pin: The task asks to allocate limited treatment capacity to maximize incremental recovery, using randomized data with covariates.

## Actuarial Science & Insurance (`actuarial-science-insurance`)

### frequency-severity-aggregate-loss-modeling (taxonomy task type: claims-frequency-severity)
1. **Frequency needs an exposure offset.** Policies are in force for different fractions of the year. Frequency models must use a Poisson or negative binomial with log earned exposure as an offset, otherwise partial-year policies look safer and rating factors that correlate with term length absorb the difference. Shortcut: Model claim count per policy record without accounting for time on risk. Pin: The prompt defines frequency as claims per earned exposure year and the data shows varying policy durations.
2. **Severity is censored at limits, truncated at deductibles.** Paid losses stop at the policy limit and losses below the deductible never appear. Severity must be fitted by a likelihood treating capped claims as censored and the deductible as left truncation; fitting observed paid amounts understates the tail and the ground-up mean. Shortcut: Fit a lognormal directly to the recorded paid amounts. Pin: The prompt asks for the ground-up severity distribution and the data records limits and deductibles per claim.
3. **Aggregate tail comes from compounding, not normal.** Annual aggregate loss is a compound sum of a random count of skewed severities. Its upper percentiles must come from Panjer recursion, FFT or simulation with the fitted overdispersed count; a normal approximation using mean and variance understates the tail. Shortcut: Take mean plus a z-score times the standard deviation as the high percentile. Pin: The question asks for a stated high percentile of the annual aggregate distribution from the fitted frequency and severity.

### pricing-rating-credibility (taxonomy task type: pricing-ratemaking-credibility)

Shortcut: Set each cell's rate from its own loss ratio, however few claims it has. Pin: The prompt names the credibility method, full-credibility standard or variance components, and the complement of credibility.
2. **Develop, trend and on-level before indicating.** Experience loss ratios must use losses developed to ultimate and trended to the future period, divided by premium restated at current rates. Using historical earned premium mixes old rate levels into the indication and misstates the needed change. Shortcut: Divide reported losses by historical earned premium and compare with the target. ### loss-reserving-claims-development (taxonomy task type: reserving-development-triangles)

Fitting a decay curve to the age-to-age factors and extrapolating captures remaining development; assuming development stops at the last observed age understates every year's reserve. 3. **Reserve range needs process and parameter error.** Reserve uncertainty combines process variance and parameter uncertainty in the factors, plus the tail. Shortcut: Quote the range between paid and incurred chain-ladder estimates as reserve uncertainty. ### catastrophe-accumulation-risk (taxonomy task type: catastrophe-exposure-accumulation)
1. **Occurrence and aggregate exceedance answer different questions.** OEP uses each year's largest event; AEP uses the year's total cat loss. Per-occurrence treaties and single-event limits are sized on OEP, annual capital and aggregate covers on AEP; mixing them misstates the return-period loss. 3. **Hotspots are tail contributors, not biggest values.** Concentration risk is where losses co-occur in the tail. Hotspots must be ranked by contribution to the stated tail metric, such as each zone's share of TVaR, because large insured values in low-hazard or uncorrelated zones add little to the tail. ### survival-lapse-policyholder-behavior (taxonomy task type: mortality-longevity)

### solvency-capital-reinsurance-optimization (taxonomy task type: reinsurance-capital)

### mortality-longevity-modeling (taxonomy task type: mortality-longevity)

## General Statistics & Data Science (`general-statistics-data-science`)

### exploratory-data-analysis-distribution-assessment
1. **Judge outliers on the right scale.** Whether a point is anomalous depends on the scale where the data are roughly symmetric. On heavily right-skewed data, mean plus three SD flags legitimate tail values; robust or log-scale rules flag the genuine departures. Shortcut: Flag every value beyond three standard deviations of the raw mean. Pin: The prompt states the anomaly rule or the distributional assumption, so the flagged set is fixed.
2. **Stratify before reading a correlation.** A pooled association can reverse inside every stratum when group sizes differ (Simpson's paradox). Profiling must check the relationship within the grouping that generates the data before calling it a real signal. Shortcut: Report the pooled correlation or trend as the dataset's finding. Pin: The question names the comparison within groups or the grouping that defines units, fixing the stratified estimand.
3. **Test modality, do not read histogram bins.** Apparent bimodality appears or vanishes as histogram bin width and origin change. Whether a variable is multimodal must be decided by a formal criterion, such as one versus two component mixture fit by BIC or a dip test, not eyeballed peaks. Shortcut: Plot a default histogram, see two bumps, and declare two distinct subpopulations. Pin: The question asks whether the population is bimodal and names the decision criterion, such as BIC or a dip test at alpha.

### regression-classification-model-diagnostics
1. **Influence needs leverage and residual together.** A point moves the fit when it has both high leverage and a large residual, measured by Cook's distance or DFBETAS. High-leverage points that fall on the line, or large-residual points at the center, change little. Shortcut: Drop every point with a large raw residual and refit. Pin: The prompt asks which observations change the coefficients, a stated influence measure with a threshold.
2. **Use leave-out residuals for misfit.** Raw residuals understate misfit at high-leverage points because the fit chases them. Studentised or leave-one-out residuals reveal the curvature or heteroskedasticity, and robust standard errors are needed when variance changes with the mean. Shortcut: Inspect raw residuals and keep classical standard errors. Pin: A stated diagnostic standard (externally studentised residuals, HC-type errors) fixes the conclusion.
3. **High AUC does not mean calibrated probabilities.** Discrimination and calibration are separate properties: AUC is unchanged by any monotone distortion of predicted risk. When decisions use absolute probabilities against a threshold, models must be judged on held-out calibration, via intercept, slope or reliability bins; a ranking metric can pick the wrong model. Shortcut: Choose the model with the higher AUC and use its scores directly as probabilities. Pin: The stated use applies a fixed probability cutoff or an expected-count target, so agreement of predicted and observed risk decides.

### resampling-simulation-monte-carlo-methods
1. **Resample the independent unit.** A bootstrap is valid only when it resamples the units that are independent: clusters, subjects or blocks of a time series. Resampling individual rows of correlated data understates variance and produces intervals that undercover. Shortcut: Resample rows with replacement regardless of grouping or time order. Pin: The data's design (repeated measures, clusters, serial dependence) dictates the resampling unit.
2. **Pick the interval for a biased statistic.** For skewed or biased nonlinear statistics, percentile intervals undercover; bias-corrected and accelerated intervals or studentised bootstrap restore coverage. A simulation from a known truth is how the coverage claim is checked. Shortcut: Take the 2.5th and 97.5th bootstrap percentiles and call coverage verified. Pin: The prompt requires verified coverage at a stated nominal level, which the simulation must demonstrate.
3. **Permute only what the null makes exchangeable.** A permutation test is exact only if labels are exchangeable under the null. Paired or stratified designs need permutation within pairs or strata, and testing one coefficient with nuisance covariates needs residual permutation such as Freedman-Lane. Shuffling all outcomes tests a different, stronger null. Shortcut: Shuffle the outcome across all rows and compare the observed statistic with that permutation distribution. Pin: The prompt states the null hypothesis and the design (pairing, strata or covariates), which fixes the valid permutation scheme.

### bayesian-modeling-posterior-inference
1. **Partial pooling, not raw or grand means.** Sparse group estimates should be shrunk toward the population mean in proportion to their noise, via a hierarchical model. Raw group means overstate extreme small groups; complete pooling erases real differences. Shortcut: Rank groups by their raw estimates, or replace all with the overall mean. Pin: The prompt asks for a hierarchical model on sparse groups, so partial pooling is the specified estimator.
2. **Report posterior predictive, not posterior mean.** A prediction for a new observation integrates parameter uncertainty and observation noise. Intervals from the posterior of the group mean are too narrow for a new unit or a new group, which also needs between-group variance. Shortcut: Use the credible interval for the group mean as the prediction interval. Pin: The prompt asks for posterior predictions for new units or groups, defining the predictive estimand.
3. **Prior sensitivity on the variance component.** With few groups, the prior on the between-group scale drives shrinkage. A sensitivity analysis must vary that scale prior, not only the location priors, because flat-looking priors on variance can be strongly informative. Shortcut: Vary the prior on the mean and declare results robust. Pin: The prompt asks for sensitivity to priors in a sparse hierarchical model, where the scale prior is the influential one.

### missing-data-censoring-measurement-error

### structural-equilibrium-modeling

### event-history-life-course-analysis
1. **Competing events are not censoring.** When death or emigration prevents the transition, the probability of experiencing it by a given age is the cumulative incidence function. ## Psychology & Behavioral Science (`psychology-behavioral-science`)

### experimental-design-power-analysis
1. **Power the interaction, not the main effect.** In a factorial design the interaction contrast usually has a much smaller standardized effect than either main effect, especially when it only attenuates an effect. Size the study for the hypothesis being tested, which can need a far larger sample. Shortcut: Compute sample size from the main-effect d and reuse it for the interaction hypothesis. Pin: The prompt states the key hypothesis is the interaction and specifies its expected effect or cell means.
2. **Within-subject power runs on dz.** Paired and repeated-measures power depends on the correlation between conditions through dz, not the between-groups d. Converting without that correlation misstates the required sample in either direction. Shortcut: Plug a between-subjects Cohen's d from the literature into a paired-test power calculation. Pin: The design is within-subject and the task supplies or fixes the correlation between repeated measures.
3. **Group randomization inflates the sample by design effect.** When classrooms or teams are randomized, required participants grow by one plus cluster size minus one times the ICC. Powering on individuals as if independent overstates power and understates the number of groups needed. Shortcut: Compute participants per arm assuming every participant is independent. Pin: The prompt states randomization at the group level with a given ICC and group size.

### psychometric-measurement-scale-validation
1. **High alpha does not mean one factor.** Cronbach's alpha assumes equal loadings and says nothing about dimensionality; with unequal loadings it misstates reliability. Fit the factor model first, then report omega from the loadings of the retained structure. Shortcut: Report alpha above the conventional cutoff and conclude the scale is unidimensional and reliable. Pin: The task asks for model-based reliability under a congeneric model or names omega as the standard.
2. **Likert items need categorical estimators.** Items with few response categories and skew violate continuous-indicator factor analysis; Pearson correlations attenuate loadings and distort fit. Use polychoric correlations with a categorical estimator such as WLSMV and judge fit with its scaled statistics. Shortcut: Run maximum likelihood CFA on Pearson correlations of five-point items. Pin: Items are stated as ordinal with few categories and the prompt names the categorical estimator.
3. **Retain factors by parallel analysis.** The eigenvalue-greater-than-one rule keeps too many factors in item sets, which changes which items cross-load and which get dropped. Compare observed eigenvalues to those from random data of the same size and retain only factors that exceed them. Shortcut: Keep every factor with an eigenvalue above one, or eyeball the scree plot. Pin: The task names parallel analysis or a stated retention criterion before item decisions.

### statistical-inference-hypothesis-testing
1. **Stimuli are a random factor too.** When participants respond to a sample of items or stimuli and the claim generalizes to other stimuli, ignoring stimulus variance inflates Type I error. Fit a mixed model with crossed random effects for participants and stimuli. Shortcut: Average over stimuli per participant and run a paired t-test. Pin: The prompt says stimuli were sampled and the conclusion must generalize to the stimulus population.
2. **Correct across the declared family of tests.** Testing several outcomes or post hoc pairs each at alpha inflates the familywise error rate. Apply the correction the preregistration or task names, such as Holm, over exactly the declared family, which can flip borderline findings. Shortcut: Declare every comparison with p below .05 significant. Pin: The prompt or preregistration defines the hypothesis family and the correction method.
3. **Test the interaction, not two simple effects.** A claim that an effect differs between groups or conditions requires a direct test of the difference. Significant in one group and non-significant in the other is not evidence of a difference; the interaction term with its interval decides. Shortcut: Conclude the groups differ because the effect is significant in one and not significant in the other. Pin: The hypothesis is stated as a moderation or group difference in the effect, and the stated test is the interaction.

### mediation-moderation-process-modeling
1. **Indirect effects need bootstrap intervals.** The product of the a and b paths is not normally distributed, so Sobel tests are underpowered and symmetric intervals are wrong. Test the indirect effect with bootstrap or Monte Carlo intervals; mediation does not require a significant total effect. Shortcut: Follow causal-steps logic and a Sobel z test, rejecting mediation when the total effect is not significant. Pin: The task specifies a bootstrap interval with a stated number of resamples for the indirect effect.
2. **Test moderated mediation with its index.** Whether a moderator changes an indirect effect is tested by the index of moderated mediation, the interaction path times the b path, with its own interval. Comparing whether subgroup indirect effects are each significant is not a test of difference. Shortcut: Split the sample by the moderator and claim moderation because one indirect effect is significant and the other is not. Pin: The prompt asks whether the indirect path is moderated and names the conditional process model.
3. **Randomized treatment does not randomize the mediator.** Randomizing the program identifies the a path, but the mediator-to-outcome path can be confounded by traits affecting both. Adjust for pre-treatment confounders of mediator and outcome and use the stated temporal order before interpreting the indirect effect causally. Shortcut: Treat the b path as causal because the program was randomly assigned. Pin: The design names baseline covariates for adjustment and measures the mediator after treatment and before the outcome.

### meta-analysis-evidence-synthesis
1. **Pool heterogeneous studies with random effects.** When interventions and populations differ, the question is the mean of a distribution of true effects. Shortcut: Pool with fixed-effect inverse-variance weights and report I-squared as the heterogeneity answer. Pin: The prompt asks for a random-effects mean with a named tau-squared estimator and a prediction interval.
2. **Effect sizes from one study are dependent.** Several outcomes or arms from the same sample share participants and error. ### longitudinal-growth-curve-modeling
1. **Slope change needs random slopes.** Testing whether treatment changes trajectories means testing the time-by-treatment interaction in a model with random intercepts and slopes. 2. **Dropout is informative in trajectories.** Participants who worsen tend to leave, so completer analyses bias trajectories. 3. **Separate within-person from between-person effects.** A time-varying predictor mixes stable differences between people with changes within a person. ### decision-making-computational-behavioral-modeling
1. **Compare models on penalized or held-out fit.** Reinforcement-learning and prospect-theory models differ in parameter count, so raw likelihood favors the more flexible model. ### experience-sampling-intensive-longitudinal-analysis
1. **Separate within- from between-person effects.** Raw daily stress mixes stable differences between people with daily fluctuations, so one coefficient blends two effects that can differ in sign.
